Seven scenario controls

Not sure what a lever means? Select the ⓘ beside it for a simple explanation.

1
PriceSuggested range: −10% to +15%
%

The average price you charge for your products or services. Increasing price can improve profit because, if sales volume stays the same, the extra revenue does not automatically increase the direct cost of making those sales.

Think about: When did you last review your prices?

2
Sales volumeSuggested range: −20% to +30%
%

The amount you sell — products, jobs, hours or services — rather than their £ value. Selling more usually increases revenue, but it also normally increases the direct costs of delivering those extra sales.

Think about: More customers, repeat business or selling more to existing customers.

3
Cost of goods soldSuggested range: −15% to +15%
%

The direct costs of producing or delivering what you sell.

Examples:

  • Retailer — stock bought for resale.
  • Manufacturer — raw materials and components.
  • Pub or café — food and drink sold.
  • Clothing printer — garments, inks, vinyl and thread.
  • Builder — materials and job-specific subcontractors.
  • Garage — parts, oils and materials used on customer repairs.
  • Consultancy or agency — subcontractors used specifically to deliver client work.

A simple test: If you sold 20% more, would you normally need more of it?

This control lets you test changes in these direct costs, such as better supplier prices, reduced waste or more efficient purchasing.

4
OverheadsSuggested range: −15% to +15%
%

The general costs of running the business that aren't directly linked to individual sales — for example rent, insurance, administration, accountancy and software. Reducing unnecessary overheads can increase profit without increasing sales.

Think about: Recurring costs that could be reduced without damaging the business.

5
Debtor daysSuggested range: −60 to +30 days
days

The average time customers take to pay you. Reducing debtor days means getting paid sooner and can release cash tied up in unpaid invoices.

Think about: Faster invoicing, payment terms and chasing overdue accounts.

6
Inventory daysSuggested range: −90 to +30 days
days

The approximate time stock or work-in-progress is held before being sold or used. Reducing excess or slow-moving inventory can release cash tied up in the business.

Think about: Stock levels, purchasing quantities and slow-moving stock.

7
Creditor daysSuggested range: −30 to +60 days
days

The average time you take to pay suppliers. Longer agreed payment terms can keep cash in the business for longer, but suppliers should always be paid within agreed terms.

Think about: Whether better payment terms could be negotiated.

i
Reducing debtor or inventory days can release cash. Increasing creditor days can also retain cash in the business, provided supplier terms allow it.

What this scenario could mean

Your changes work together. Price, sales, costs and working capital can affect one another, so this tool looks at the combined scenario.

Profitability Navigator is an illustrative scenario-planning tool. Results are based on simplified assumptions and the figures entered. They are not forecasts and do not constitute accounting, tax, investment or financial advice. Actual business outcomes may differ. Prepared for the client by the adviser.
